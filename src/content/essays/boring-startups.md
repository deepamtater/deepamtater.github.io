Every few months someone asks me what kind of startup they should start, and every few months I give the same unsatisfying answer: pick the idea that sounds the least impressive at dinner. Not because boring is a virtue, but because boring is a filter. It keeps out the competitors who are optimizing for how the company sounds instead of how it works.

## The dinner-party test

Try describing your idea to a stranger. If their eyes light up, be a little worried. Exciting ideas attract exciting money, and exciting money attracts a crowd. The crowd doesn't need to be better than you to hurt you; it only needs to be loud enough that customers can't hear you.

When the stranger's eyes glaze over instead, you've learned something useful. You've found a problem that is real to the people who have it and invisible to everyone else. That invisibility is a moat you didn't have to build.

> The best problems are the ones people have stopped complaining about, because they assume nothing can be done.

## Why boring compounds

Boring markets have three properties that compound in a founder's favor:

- **Customers stay.** People don't switch payroll vendors for fun. Once you're in, you're in.
- **Feedback is honest.** Nobody uses an invoicing tool ironically. If they keep paying, it works.
- **The bar is low.** Incumbents in unglamorous categories often haven't shipped a meaningful change in a decade.[^1]

None of this makes the work easier. It makes the work _count_. Effort in a crowded market gets diluted by everyone else's effort. Effort in a quiet market accumulates.

## The trap on the other side

There is a failure mode here, and it's worth naming. Some problems are boring because they're small. The test isn't whether an idea is dull; it's whether it's dull _and_ painful _and_ widespread. A good boring idea makes a specific group of people visibly relieved when you describe it. If they shrug, you've found a hobby, not a company.

---

What I'm really arguing for is a kind of indifference to status. The founders I admire most picked the problem first and let the story come later. Usually, once the company worked, the story turned out to be interesting after all.

[^1]: This is a generalization, and like all generalizations it's wrong somewhere. But it's right often enough to be worth checking.
